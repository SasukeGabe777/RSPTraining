▶️ ACROSS-THE-LINE (DOL) STARTER

Full voltage.
Instant start.

The Across-the-Line starter — also called DOL (Direct-On-Line) — applies full voltage instantly to the motor. It's the most common starter type, the lowest cost, and the simplest design: a contactor and an overload relay, period.

⚡
DOL / ATL STARTER
CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD · FULL-VOLTAGE START
What's in the box
  • Contactor — switches motor power on/off
  • Overload relay — sized to motor FLA
  • (Upstream breaker or fuses live outside the DOL starter)
Pros · Cons
  • ✓ Best forSmall to medium motors, low-inertia loads, strong power systems
  • ✗ Bad forHigh inertia, weak power supplies, sensitive mechanical systems

⚠️ The inrush trade-off

DOL is cheap and simple — but it hits the motor with 6–8× full-load amps at startup. On a small motor this is fine. On a 50 HP pump on a weak transformer, it can dim the lights and trip the upstream breaker. That's when reps reach for a soft starter or VFD.

💡 POWER-UP: "DOL = full voltage, instant on." If the customer hasn't mentioned inrush, speed, or reversing — DOL is the default quote.

▶️ REVERSING STARTER

Two contactors.
One interlock.

A reversing starter spins a motor in either direction by swapping any two of the three line phases. The trick: you need two contactors (one for forward, one for reverse) and an interlock that guarantees they can never close at the same time — because closing both means a phase-to-phase short.

🔄
REVERSING STARTER
2 CONTACTORS + INTERLOCK + OVERLOAD
What's in the box
  • Forward contactor + reverse contactor (wired to swap two phases)
  • Mechanical interlock — physically blocks both from closing at once
  • Electrical interlock — extra layer; aux contacts cross-wired into the opposing coil circuit
  • Single overload relay downstream — sized to motor FLA
Typical applications
  • Hoists, cranes, gates, conveyors with reversing requirement
  • Machine tools (drill presses, lathes that need reverse jog)

📞 The qualifying question

When a customer asks for "forward/reverse," confirm whether they want electrical interlock only (cheaper) or mechanical + electrical (industrial standard, safer). Most quoting mistakes here come from skipping that question.

💡 POWER-UP: "FWD/REV means two contactors plus an interlock. Always ask if mechanical interlock is required — it usually is."

▶️ MANUAL vs MAGNETIC STARTERS

Hand on the lever
or coil on a wire.

Two starter types, same job — switch the motor and protect it from overload. The difference is how the switching happens.

🖐️
MANUAL MOTOR STARTER
MECHANICAL SWITCH + BUILT-IN OVERLOAD · LOCAL CONTROL ONLY
A toggle, rocker, or push-button mechanically opens and closes the motor contacts. Overload protection is built into the switch body. No control coil, no remote start.
Pros · Cons
  • ✓ Best forSmall fractional-HP motors, single-pole tool circuits, no-automation jobs
  • ✗ Bad forAnything PLC-driven, remote start/stop, or interlocked automation
🧲
MAGNETIC MOTOR STARTER
CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD · ELECTRICALLY CONTROLLED
A control coil (24V / 120V / 230V) pulls a contactor closed. Energize the coil = motor runs. Drop the coil = motor stops. The standard for any system with remote pushbuttons, PLC outputs, interlocks, or automation.
Pros · Cons
  • ✓ Best forRemote/PLC control, automated lines, anything requiring seal-in logic
  • ✗ Bad forStandalone hand tools where a wall switch is enough
💡 POWER-UP: "Manual = hand. Magnetic = coil." If anyone says "PLC" or "remote start," you're quoting magnetic — every time.

▶️ COMBINATION & MCP STARTERS

Disconnect.
Short-circuit.
Start. Protect.

When code, panel design, or MCC layout calls for "the complete package," you're quoting a combination starter or its close cousin the MCP starter. Both wrap the four motor-circuit jobs into one device.

📦
COMBINATION STARTER
DISCONNECT + BREAKER/FUSE + CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD · ONE ASSEMBLY
What's in the box
  • Disconnect switch — safety isolation for service
  • Short-circuit protection — breaker (MCCB) or fuse block
  • Contactor — motor switching
  • Overload relay — sized to FLA
Why customers want it
  • Meets NEC requirements for full motor protection in one UL-listed assembly
  • Simplifies panel design — fewer separate parts, fewer wires
  • Faster installation, cleaner documentation
🪣
MCP STARTER (MOTOR CIRCUIT PROTECTOR)
MCP + CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD · MCC BUCKET STANDARD
An MCP is a magnetic-only circuit protector — short-circuit protection, no thermal trip. It MUST be paired with a separate overload relay. The classic MCC (Motor Control Center) bucket configuration.

🚨 The MCP rule

MCP without an overload relay = unprotected motor. Always confirm both are quoted together. "I need a bucket" almost always means MCP + contactor + OL.

💡 POWER-UP: Combination = "all-in-one starter with breaker." MCP = "MCC bucket flavor — magnetic-only, must add OL."

▶️ SOFT STARTER & VFD AS STARTER ALTERNATIVES

When the starter
isn't enough.

Three devices can all "start a motor" — but only one of them controls speed. Knowing the difference is the single biggest sales-skill gap in motor control.

FeatureDOL StarterSoft StarterVFD
Start motor✅✅✅
Reduce inrush❌✅✅
Speed control❌❌✅
Cost💲💲💲💲💲💲
ComplexityLowMediumHigh
Built-in overload?No (external OL)SometimesYes (built-in)

📞 The sales rule

If the customer needs speed control, a starter — any starter — is not enough. Quote a VFD. If they only need to reduce inrush or smooth out mechanical shock, a soft starter is the right answer. Otherwise, DOL is the default.

🚨 The most common mistake

Reps assume "starter" reduces inrush. It doesn't. A DOL starter applies full voltage instantly — inrush is unchanged. Reducing inrush requires a soft starter, VFD, or reduced-voltage starter (autotransformer / star-delta / primary resistance).

💡 POWER-UP: "Speed = VFD. Soft start = soft starter. Otherwise = starter." Three sentences, every motor-control quote.

▶️ COMPONENTS INSIDE A STARTER

Seven parts.
Memorize them.

Every starter is built from the same parts kit. Knowing which parts appear in which starter is what separates "rep" from "engineer-grade quote."

ComponentFunctionAppears in
ContactorSwitches motor power on/offAll starters except manual
Overload Relay (OL)Protects motor from sustained overcurrent / heatAll starters except VFD (built-in)
Breaker or FusesShort-circuit protectionCombination starters, MCC feeders
MCPMagnetic-only short-circuit protectionMCP starters / MCC buckets
DisconnectSafety isolationCombination starters, many service starters
InterlockPrevents both contactors closing at onceReversing starters
Control TransformerDrops 480V → 120V control voltageIndustrial magnetic starters
Aux ContactsStatus feedback, seal-in, PLC signalingOptional add-on; common in industrial starters

📌 The two pillars

Contactor = switching. Overload = motor protection. Everything else (disconnect, breaker, transformer, aux contacts, interlock) is convenience packaging around those two functions.

💡 POWER-UP: Read the parts list off the table when quoting — out loud. It catches the "wait, do they have a control transformer?" question before the customer has to ask.

▶️ OVERLOAD RELAYS · SIZING TO FLA

Size by FLA.
Never by HP alone.

This is the most-asked, most-mis-answered sizing question on the phone. The overload protects the motor — and the only number that tells the truth about a motor's current is its nameplate FLA.

📋 The 3-step rule

1. Find the motor's Full Load Amps (FLA) on the nameplate.
2. Pick an overload whose adjustable range covers that FLA.
3. Set the dial to the FLA reading — and pick the right trip class (10 for standard, 20–30 for high inertia).

🚫 UNDERSIZED

  • ✗ Trips on every motor start ("nuisance trips")
  • ✗ Reps swap to a bigger overload to silence the alarm
  • ✗ Motor is now under-protected — back to square one
  • ✗ Customer thinks the part is defective

🔥 OVERSIZED ("to be safe")

  • ✗ Motor cooks before the overload notices
  • ✗ Insurance / warranty fight after the fact
  • ✗ "I sized it to be safe" is the actual cause
  • ✗ The most expensive mistake in motor control

⚠️ Thermal vs Electronic

Thermal OLs use bimetal strips that bend with heat — simple, reliable, no diagnostics, often fixed Class 10. Electronic OLs use current transformers + an IC — programmable trip class, phase-loss detection, comms (Modbus / EtherNet/IP). Use electronic when the customer needs remote diagnostics or has VFD/process loads.

💡 POWER-UP: "Send me the motor nameplate" beats "send me the HP" every time. Get the FLA.

🛠️ NEMA vs IEC STARTERS

Two philosophies.
Two pricing tiers.

Every starter SKU you'll ever quote is either NEMA (North American, conservative, oversized) or IEC (European, compact, precise). Knowing which philosophy fits the application is half the sale.

CategoryNEMAIEC
SizingBroad steps (Size 00, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)Precisely sized to motor FLA
DurabilityHeavy-duty, built for decadesCompact, replaceable
Overload adjustabilityWide rangeNarrow range (sized to motor)
CostHigherLower
PhilosophyConservative — safe to oversizePrecise — must match exact rating
Common brandsEaton, Allen-Bradley, Square DSchneider, Siemens, ABB
Best forHarsh North American industrial plantsOEM panels, export equipment

📞 The sales question

"Are you matching an existing NEMA frame, or designing a new panel?" If matching existing → stay NEMA. If new OEM design → IEC saves cost and panel space. Don't mix philosophies in the same starter.

💡 POWER-UP: NEMA = American muscle, easy to oversize. IEC = European precision, must size exact. Both protect motors; they just do it differently.

🛠️ STARTER SIZING BY MOTOR HP

Five buckets.
Five answers.

Motor HP isn't the only number that matters (FLA still rules the overload sizing), but it's the fastest way to put the customer in the right starter family on the first call.

Motor HPTypical Starter TypeUpstream Protection
0.25 – 3 HPManual or DOL starterMCB or small MCCB
3 – 10 HPDOL or reversing starterMCCB + OL relay
10 – 30 HPCombination starter or soft starterMCP or MCCB combo
30 – 100 HPSoft starter, combo starter, or VFDVerify SCCR — fuses for high fault current
100+ HPVFD package or MCC combinationAlmost always fused disconnect or high-AIC MCCB

⚠️ Sizing pitfalls

1. HP alone doesn't tell you FLA — voltage and service factor change it. 2. "Bumping up one size" sounds safe but mis-sizes the overload. 3. Above 30 HP, always check the SCCR (Short-Circuit Current Rating) of the assembly — code requires it.

📌 SCCR — the forgotten rating

SCCR is the highest fault current a starter assembly can interrupt without damage. Modern industrial sites can have 25–100 kA available; if your starter's SCCR is 5 kA, it's a code violation. Fuses give the highest SCCR cheaply, which is why fused combination starters dominate above 30 HP.

💡 POWER-UP: Memorize the five HP buckets. The customer says "10 HP pump" and you instantly know they're in the combination/soft-starter zone — not a bare DOL.

🛠️ PROTECTION MATRIX

Overload OR short-circuit?
Always both.

A motor needs two kinds of protection: slow-cooker (overload) and blow-up (short-circuit). Different starters put them in different places. This matrix tells you what's missing.

Starter TypeOverload Protection?Short-Circuit Protection?Where SC lives
DOL StarterYes (OL relay)NoUpstream breaker / fuses
Reversing StarterYes (OL relay)NoUpstream breaker / fuses
Soft StarterYes / No (model-dependent)NoUpstream breaker / fuses
VFD StarterYes (built-in)NoUpstream MCCB or fuses
Combination StarterYesYesInside the assembly
MCP StarterYes (external OL)Yes (MCP)Inside the assembly
Manual StarterYes (built-in)NoUpstream breaker / fuses

📌 The mental model

A contactor switches. An overload protects the motor. A breaker or fuse protects the wiring and handles short circuits. Three jobs, three devices. A "starter" packages the first two; the third is either upstream or bundled into a combination/MCP assembly.

🚨 The "missing protection" trap

A customer orders a bare DOL starter and assumes it's "fully protected." It isn't — short-circuit protection lives outside the starter. If the upstream breaker is wrong (or missing), the first ground fault welds the contactor shut. Always confirm what's upstream when quoting a non-combination starter.

💡 POWER-UP: Two protections, two devices, two places. Memorize where each one lives for every starter type.

🛠️ STARTER ACCESSORIES

The five
upsells that matter.

Five accessories show up on almost every industrial starter quote. Missing them costs reorders, callbacks, and trust. Offer them by default unless the customer says no.

AccessoryPurposeOffer when…
Aux ContactsStatus feedback to PLC / remote displayCustomer mentions PLC, SCADA, remote monitoring
Control TransformerDrops 480V → 120V for the contactor coilIndustrial 480V starter, anytime control voltage ≠ line voltage
Pilot LightsLocal visual indication (RUN / FAULT / READY)Customer wants operator panel feedback
Start/Stop StationsRemote pushbutton controlCustomer has field operators; motor isn't right next to the starter
NEMA EnclosureEnvironmental protection (1 / 3R / 4 / 4X / 12)Outdoor, washdown, dusty, or harsh location

📞 The "Did you order…" follow-ups

Three phrases close every starter call:
1. "Do you need a control transformer for the coil voltage?"
2. "Pilot lights and start/stop, or just the bare starter?"
3. "What enclosure rating — indoor clean, dusty, washdown, or outdoor?"

⚠️ The enclosure rule

Open chassis is rated for installation inside another enclosure. NEMA 1 = clean indoor. NEMA 12 = dusty industrial. NEMA 4/4X = washdown / outdoor (4X is corrosion-resistant for food & chemical). Never quote an open chassis to a customer installing in a standalone outdoor location.

💡 POWER-UP: The accessories ARE the quote. A bare starter + the right five accessories doubles the ticket and makes the install actually work.

🛠️ QUICK DECISION PATH

Six questions.
One starter.

Run these six questions in order on every motor-control call. The answer to each one narrows the SKU. By the last question you're at one starter type — guaranteed.

1. Need variable speed?
YES → VFD (not a starter)
NO → continue
2. Tripping on startup (inrush)?
YES → Soft Starter or VFD
NO → continue
3. Replacing an MCC bucket?
YES → MCP Starter
NO → continue
4. Need overload + short-circuit in one unit?
YES → Combination Starter
NO → continue
5. Need forward / reverse?
YES → Reversing Starter
NO → continue
6. Small fractional HP, no automation?
YES → Manual Starter
NO → DOL Starter (default)

📌 Highest SCCR variant

If the answer is "combination" AND the customer mentions high fault current (over 25 kA available), upgrade to a fused combination starter. Fuses give the highest SCCR for the lowest dollar.

💡 POWER-UP: Print this decision path. Tape it next to the phone. Run it every call until it's muscle memory — then your quotes never miss.

🛠️ 5 SALES MISTAKES TO AVOID

Each one costs
an order.

Every mistake on this list has cost a rep a callback, a returned part, or — worst case — a burned-up motor. Memorize and avoid.

1

Thinking a starter reduces inrush.

A DOL starter applies full voltage instantly. Inrush is unchanged. Reducing inrush requires a soft starter, VFD, or reduced-voltage method (autotransformer / star-delta).

2

Confusing a starter with a drive.

A starter does not control speed. If the customer needs variable speed, you're quoting a VFD — full stop. Selling them a "starter" for variable-speed is a returned part and a lost relationship.

3

Missing overload sizing.

Overloads MUST be sized to motor FLA, not HP. Two motors at the same HP can have different FLAs depending on voltage and service factor. Always ask for the nameplate.

4

Selling a DOL into a weak power system.

DOL starters hit the line with 6–8× FLA. On a weak transformer or rural service, that drops voltage on the rest of the plant — and trips the upstream breaker. Reach for a soft starter or reduced-voltage starter.

5

Forgetting the accessories.

Control transformer · pilot lights · start/stop stations · aux contacts · proper enclosure. The bare starter never ships alone — and forgetting one means a callback the next day.

▶️ What a starter IS

STARTER = CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD RELAY (with short-circuit protection upstream, unless it's a combination/MCP starter — then SC is in the box). Three jobs: start, stop, protect. No speed control.

🧰 The 7 starter types

DOL full-voltage default · Reversing two contactors + interlock · Manual small motors, no automation · Magnetic coil-controlled, remote/PLC · Combination disconnect+breaker+contactor+OL · MCP MCC bucket (MCP + contactor + OL) · Soft Starter / VFD if inrush or speed are issues.

📞 The 5 first questions

1) Motor voltage, HP, FLA · 2) Control coil voltage · 3) Variable speed needed? · 4) Forward/reverse? · 5) NEMA or IEC frame?

🛡️ Overload sizing

✔ Size to motor FLA (not HP) · ✔ Trip Class 10 standard, 20–30 high inertia · ✔ Adjustable range must cover FLA. ❌ Never oversize "to be safe."

📐 NEMA vs IEC

NEMA conservative, heavy-duty, sized in steps (00–6), safe to oversize. IEC compact, precise, sized to exact FLA, cheaper, must match ratings.

🪣 Customer phrase decoder

"I need a starter" → DOL · "Full protection" → Combination · "A bucket" → MCP · "Forward/reverse" → Reversing · "Trips on startup" → Soft Starter / VFD · "Adjustable speed" → VFD.

🚨 5 Mistakes to Avoid

Thinking a starter reduces inrush · Confusing starter with VFD · Missing FLA-based overload sizing · DOL on a weak power system · Forgetting accessories (CT, pilot lights, start/stop, aux contacts, enclosure).

🧠 The Memory Hook

A starter starts and protects. It doesn't reduce inrush. It doesn't control speed. If either of those is needed, you're past the starter — soft starter or VFD.
