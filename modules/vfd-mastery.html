⚡ WHAT A VFD DOES

Control the frequency.
Control the speed.

A Variable Frequency Drive converts fixed-frequency AC power into variable-frequency, variable-voltage AC output. By changing the frequency, you change the motor speed. That's the whole game.

⚡ The power conversion path

Utility ACFixed Hz, Fixed V
→
RectifierAC → DC
→
DC BusStores energy
→
Inverter (PWM)DC → Variable AC
→
MotorAny Hz you set

📐 Speed formula

RPM ≈ (120 × Hz) / motor poles

4-pole motor at 60 Hz → 1800 RPM
4-pole motor at 30 Hz → 900 RPM

💰 Why VFDs save energy

For fans and pumps, power drops by the cube of speed. A fan at 50% speed uses only 12.5% of full power. That's the sales pitch.

📋 What VFDs are used for

Energy savings on fans & pumps · Soft starting (eliminates 6–8× inrush) · Variable speed on conveyors, mixers, compressors · Process control (maintain pressure, flow, tension) · Reduced mechanical stress and longer motor life.

💡 POWER-UP: "A VFD controls frequency and voltage — not just speed. Frequency drives the motor shaft; voltage keeps the motor magnetized at the right level."

⚡ THE 3 NON-NEGOTIABLE QUESTIONS

Ask these three.
Before quoting anything.

Every VFD mis-quote traces back to skipping one of these three categories. Stop, ask, confirm — then quote.

🔌
1. ELECTRICAL REQUIREMENTS
Input power · Control voltage · Short-circuit current
  • Input voltage: 208 / 230 / 460 / 575 VAC
  • Phase: single-phase or three-phase (derating required for single-phase — see Page 7)
  • Available short-circuit current (SCCR requirements)
  • Control voltage needed (24 VDC, 120 VAC, or built-in keypad only?)
⚙️
2. MOTOR DETAILS
HP · FLA · RPM · Enclosure · Inverter-duty?
  • Horsepower (HP) and Full Load Amps (FLA) — FLA drives the sizing
  • Motor RPM and number of poles
  • Motor enclosure: TEFC, ODP, washdown
  • Is it inverter-duty rated? (critical for continuous low-speed operation)
🏭
3. APPLICATION TYPE
Load type · Environment · Duty cycle · Control needs
  • Constant torque or variable torque? (most important — see Page 6)
  • Environment: clean, dusty, washdown, outdoor, corrosive
  • Duty cycle: continuous, intermittent, reversing
  • Control sophistication: simple potentiometer speed vs PLC analog input vs fieldbus
💡 POWER-UP: "If you don't know all three — stop and ask before quoting. A VFD returned for wrong voltage or wrong torque class costs everyone time and trust."

⚡ CONSTANT vs VARIABLE TORQUE

The single most important
VFD concept.

Get this wrong and you undersize the drive. Get it right and you save the customer thousands of dollars per year in energy costs.

🔴 CONSTANT TORQUE

Torque stays constant as speed changes. Motor must deliver full torque even at low speed.

  • 🏭 Conveyors & belts
  • 🔩 Extruders
  • 🥣 Mixers & agitators
  • 💧 Positive displacement pumps
  • 🏗️ Hoists & cranes
⚠️ Do NOT undersize · May need to oversize · Inverter-duty motor required for low-speed operation

🟠 VARIABLE TORQUE

Torque decreases as speed decreases. Power drops by the cube of speed.

  • 🌬️ Fans & blowers
  • 💧 Centrifugal pumps
  • 🌀 Centrifugal compressors
  • 🌊 Circulators
✅ Drive may be smaller · Massive energy savings possible · Most HVAC and pump applications

⚡ The cube law — the most powerful sales fact about VFDs

A centrifugal pump or fan at 50% speed uses only 12.5% of full power (0.5³ = 0.125). On a 50 HP fan motor running at 50% speed, that's saving over 43 HP of energy — continuously. This is why VFDs pay for themselves in months on fan and pump applications.

💡 POWER-UP: "Conveyor = constant torque, size up if in doubt. Fan or pump = variable torque, biggest energy-savings play in the catalog."

⚡ INPUT POWER & DERATING RULES

Single phase in.
Oversize the drive.

Most VFDs are designed and rated for three-phase input. When a customer has only single-phase power available, you must oversize the drive — or it will fail early.

✅ Three-Phase Input

No derating required. Standard sizing rules apply: match VFD FLA rating to motor FLA. This is the normal case for industrial applications.

⚠️ Single-Phase Input

DC bus ripple nearly doubles. Heat rises. Most VFDs designed for 3-phase will fail prematurely on single-phase without derating.

📐 The Derating Rule of Thumb

For a three-phase VFD on single-phase input: oversize the drive by 1.73× (√3), or select the next drive size up.

Example: Customer needs to drive a 10 HP, 28A motor on single-phase 230V. Minimum VFD size = 28A × 1.73 = ~48A rated drive (approximately 15–20 HP class).

🚨 What happens without derating

· DC bus capacitors overheat and fail early
· Nuisance overcurrent faults at normal loads
· Drive lifespan drops from 10+ years to 1–2 years
· Warranty voided — manufacturer will find the single-phase cause

✅ The exception: single-phase-rated VFDs

Some drives are explicitly designed and rated for single-phase input. These have heavier bus capacitors and different thermal management. Always verify the datasheet — "accepts single-phase" and "rated for single-phase" are not the same thing.

💡 POWER-UP: "Single phase = oversize 1.73× or step up to the next size. This is a hard rule — no exceptions for standard drives."

⚡ MOTOR + VFD COMPATIBILITY

Not every motor
survives a VFD.

The VFD doesn't care what motor you attach — but the motor cares. Continuous low-speed operation, high carrier frequencies, and long cable runs can destroy a motor that isn't rated for VFD service.

Motor TypeVFD SuitabilityLow-Speed UseNotes
Inverter-Duty✅ Best✅ Any speedReinforced insulation, self-cooling at any speed, built for VFD service
Standard TEFC⚠️ Limited⚠️ Above ~40 Hz onlyCooling fan slows with motor — risk of overheating below 30 Hz on constant loads
ODP Motor❌ Poor❌ AvoidCooling depends entirely on shaft speed; open frame admits dust in dirty environments
Washdown Motor✅ If inverter-duty✅ YesCommon in food/pharma — confirm it is also inverter-duty rated, not just washdown-rated

⚡ Long cable runs: the reflected wave problem

VFD output is PWM — fast voltage pulses, not a true sine wave. Cable runs over 50–100 ft act like transmission lines. The impedance mismatch at the motor terminals causes voltage to reflect back and double. On a 460V system (DC bus ~650V), the reflected wave can reach 1,200–1,300V peak at the motor terminals — far above standard insulation ratings.

🛡️ Solutions for long cable runs

· Load reactor (output reactor): protects motor at runs 50–200 ft
· dv/dt filter: limits the rate of voltage rise; best for 100–500 ft
· Sine wave filter: produces near-true sine wave output; longest runs and most sensitive motors

💡 POWER-UP: "Inverter-duty motor + VFD = designed pair. Standard TEFC + VFD = workable above 40 Hz. ODP + VFD = avoid. Long leads? Add output accessories."

⚡ CARRIER FREQUENCY & ENCLOSURE SELECTION

How it sounds.
Where it lives.

Two settings that are often overlooked but matter for every installation: how fast the VFD switches its output, and which enclosure protects the electronics.

Carrier Frequency Tradeoff
SettingMotor SoundDrive Heat
High (>8 kHz)QuietMore heat
Low (2–4 kHz)Audible humLess heat
Lower carrier frequency → longer drive life. Raise it only if motor noise is a customer complaint.
VFD Enclosure Selection
EnclosureEnvironment
Open / ChassisInside clean control panels only
NEMA 1Clean indoor — offices, clean rooms
NEMA 12Dusty industrial — machine shops
NEMA 4Washdown, rain-protected outdoor
NEMA 4XWashdown + corrosion resistant (food, chemical)

🌡️ Heat is the hidden enclosure problem

VFDs generate 3–5% of their rated power as heat. An open chassis VFD in a NEMA 12 panel needs panel fans or heat exchangers — it can't rely on the same ventilation slots the open-frame drive expects. Always confirm the enclosure thermal plan, not just the NEMA rating.

💡 POWER-UP: "Open chassis = inside another enclosure only. NEMA 12 = dusty plant floor. NEMA 4/4X = the second a hose or rain is involved."

⚡ BRAKING METHODS

Stop the motor fast.
Three ways to do it.

When a motor decelerates, its inertia keeps it spinning — that energy has to go somewhere. The braking method determines where that energy goes and how quickly the motor stops.

MethodHow It WorksEnergy Goes ToBest ForCost
DC Injection Injects DC current into motor windings to create opposing magnetic field Heat in motor windings Light loads, infrequent stops, small motors 💲 Low
Dynamic Braking Motor acts as generator during decel; energy dumped into external resistor bank Heat in braking resistor High inertia loads (conveyors, presses, saws, hoists) 💲💲 Medium
Regenerative VFD returns motor braking energy back to the AC supply line as real power Back to utility grid Large systems, frequent stop/start cycles, crane lowering 💲💲💲 High

📞 The braking sales question

Ask: "Does the load need to stop quickly, or is a gradual coast-to-stop acceptable?" Fans and centrifugal pumps almost never need braking — they coast down safely. Conveyors, hoists, and presses often do. A loaded conveyor without braking can overspeed the drive on decel and trigger overvoltage faults.

📐 Braking resistor sizing note

Dynamic braking resistors must be sized for the duty cycle, not just peak braking power. A hoist that brakes 20 times per hour needs a much larger (or better-cooled) resistor than one that brakes once per hour at the same load. Always confirm duty cycle before specifying the resistor.

💡 POWER-UP: "Dynamic braking = resistor dumps the energy as heat. Regenerative = energy goes back to the grid. Fans usually need neither. Hoists usually need dynamic."

🛠️ VFD ACCESSORIES — WHEN TO OFFER EACH

The right accessory
at the right time.

VFD accessories aren't optional upsells — they're often code requirements or application necessities. Know when each one is needed and offer it proactively.

AccessoryProtectsOffer When…
Line Reactor
(Input side)		 VFD from utility harmonics, voltage spikes, and inrush during power-up Long power runs · Weak utility · Sensitive downstream equipment · Multiple VFDs on same circuit
Load Reactor
(Output side)		 Motor from VFD output spikes; reduces reflected wave effect on medium cable runs Motor cable runs 50–200 ft · Standard TEFC motor on VFD · Intermittent overloads
dv/dt Filter
(Output side)		 Motor insulation from fast voltage rise rate (dv/dt); more aggressive than load reactor Cable runs 100–500 ft · Older motors with standard insulation · High-frequency switching
Sine Wave Filter
(Output side)		 Motor with near-perfect sine wave output; eliminates virtually all PWM stress Very long runs >500 ft · Non-inverter-duty motors · Audible motor noise complaints · Sensitive machinery
Braking Resistor
(External)		 N/A — energy dissipation device for fast controlled stops High inertia loads (saws, presses, hoists) · Loaded conveyor decel · Customer says "it needs to stop fast"
EMI/RFI Filter
(Input side)		 Surrounding equipment from VFD-generated radio frequency interference Near sensors, PLCs, medical equipment · CE compliance required · Customer mentions interference issues

🛠️ 5 SALES MISTAKES TO AVOID

Each one costs
an order.

Every mistake on this list has put a rep in a bad position — returned drive, burned motor, unhappy customer, or missed margin. Memorize and avoid.

1

Undersizing the drive.

Sizing to HP alone ignores that two motors at the same HP can have different FLAs. Always size to motor FLA. And if the load is constant torque, verify the drive's CT rating — a "10 HP VT-rated drive" may be rated 7.5 HP for constant torque use.

2

Ignoring single-phase derating.

Customer mentions single phase — you pull a standard three-phase drive of equal HP. The drive fails in 6 months. Oversize by 1.73× or use a drive explicitly rated for single-phase input. This rule has no exceptions for standard drives.

3

Not checking motor duty rating.

A standard ODP motor on a VFD in a dusty washdown plant = failed motor within a year. Ask whether the motor is inverter-duty. If it's not — and the application runs it below 40 Hz continuously — that motor needs to be replaced or a forced-cooling blower added.

4

Forgetting heat dissipation.

A 20 HP VFD in a sealed NEMA 4X enclosure generates ~300W of heat continuously. Without panel cooling (heat exchanger or AC unit), the drive will thermal-fault. Always ask: "How is the enclosure being cooled?"

5

Quoting variable torque for a constant torque load.

VT-rated drives may be derated or unrated for CT applications. A conveyor VFD quoted as "VT" and run on a constant torque load will trip on overcurrent at low speeds when torque demand is highest. Ask the load type. Every time.

🛠️ VFD SUBSTITUTION RULES

When a customer
asks for "Brand X."

VFD substitution is common — customers replace legacy drives or cross to an in-stock equivalent. These rules protect the customer and protect you from a return.

✅ ALLOWED

  • ✔ Same or higher HP / current rating
  • ✔ Same or better enclosure rating
  • ✔ Same voltage class (208V, 230V, 460V, 575V)
  • ✔ Same phase (3-phase for 3-phase)
  • ✔ Same or more advanced control features
  • ✔ Multi-voltage drives with correct jumper setting

❌ NOT ALLOWED

  • ✗ Lower HP or lower current rating
  • ✗ Wrong input voltage (even close voltages fail)
  • ✗ Ignoring single-phase derating requirement
  • ✗ Variable torque-only drive on a constant torque load
  • ✗ Enclosure downgrade (4X → 12 in washdown area)
  • ✗ Assuming features carry over (fieldbus, comm cards)

📞 The cross-reference call script

When a customer gives you a model number to cross: (1) Confirm input voltage and phase. (2) Confirm HP and FLA rating. (3) Confirm CT or VT rating. (4) Confirm enclosure type. (5) Ask about any special control interface (Profibus, DeviceNet, Modbus) — comm cards rarely swap 1:1. That's the full substitution checklist.

💡 POWER-UP: "Higher HP is safe. Lower HP is a return. Wrong voltage is a write-off. Verify all five checkpoints before you confirm a cross."

🛠️ VFD QUICK DECISION PATH

Six questions.
One VFD spec.

Run these six questions in order on every VFD call. Each answer eliminates options and narrows the spec. By the last question you have a drive and accessory list — not just a model number guess.

1. Is speed control actually needed?
YES → VFD is the right product
NO → Soft starter or DOL starter may be sufficient
2. Constant torque or variable torque?
CT → Size for full torque at low speed; don't downsize
VT → Standard sizing; biggest energy-savings play
3. Single-phase or three-phase input?
Single-phase → Oversize 1.73× or use single-phase-rated VFD
Three-phase → Standard sizing applies
4. Is the motor inverter-duty rated?
YES → Full speed range, any carrier freq
NO → Limit low-speed operation; consider load reactor or motor upgrade
5. Motor cable run over 50 ft?
YES → Add load reactor (50–200 ft) or dv/dt filter (100–500 ft)
NO → Standard connection, no output accessories required
6. Outdoor, washdown, or dusty?
Washdown/outdoor → NEMA 4 / 4X; plan for drive heat dissipation
Dusty indoor → NEMA 12; Clean indoor → NEMA 1 or chassis in panel

📌 Bonus question: does it need to stop fast?

If yes — "What is the load?" Conveyors, hoists, or high-inertia machines often need a dynamic braking resistor. Quote it before the customer finds out the hard way after a drive overvoltage fault on first decel test.

✂ TEAR HERE

⚡ What a VFD does

Converts fixed-frequency AC → variable-frequency/voltage AC output. Controls motor speed (via Hz) and torque (via V/Hz ratio). Power path: Utility → Rectifier → DC Bus → Inverter (PWM) → Motor.

🔴🟠 Constant vs Variable Torque — the critical split

CT (constant torque): Conveyors, extruders, mixers, PD pumps, hoists → full torque at low speed → don't undersize → inverter-duty motor required.
VT (variable torque): Fans, centrifugal pumps, blowers → torque drops with speed → huge energy savings (cube law) → may downsize drive.

📐 Single-phase derating

3-phase VFD on single-phase input: oversize by 1.73× (or next size up). No exceptions for standard drives. Failure = DC bus overheating and early failure.

🔧 Motor compatibility quick guide

Inverter-duty: ✅ Any speed, any carrier freq. Standard TEFC: ⚠️ Above ~40 Hz only. ODP: ❌ Avoid on VFD service. Long leads (>50 ft): add output reactor or dv/dt filter.

🌡️ VFD enclosures

Open/chassis = inside panel. NEMA 1 = clean indoor. NEMA 12 = dusty plant. NEMA 4/4X = washdown or outdoor. Drives generate heat — plan thermal management in closed enclosures.

🛠️ Accessories when to offer

Line reactor: Input protection, harmonics. Load reactor: Output, medium cable runs 50–200 ft. dv/dt filter: 100–500 ft or older motors. Sine wave filter: Long runs, sensitive motors. Braking resistor: High inertia, fast stops. EMI filter: Near sensors/PLCs.

📞 The 5 first questions

1) Load type (fan, pump, conveyor?) 2) Motor HP and FLA 3) Input voltage and phase 4) Inverter-duty motor? 5) Cable run length?

🚨 5 Mistakes to Avoid

Undersizing to HP alone · Ignoring single-phase derating · Non-inverter motor at low speed · No heat plan for sealed enclosure · VT drive on a CT load.

🧠 The Memory Hook

Fan or pump = variable torque = biggest energy savings. Conveyor or hoist = constant torque = don't undersize. Single phase = oversize. Long leads = add output accessories.
