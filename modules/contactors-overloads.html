🔌 WHY THIS TRAINING MATTERS

A starter is NOT
a single device.

Most of the confusion in motor-control sales comes from one mistake: treating a starter as a single part. It isn't. A starter is an assembly of components — and the two most important are the contactor and the overload relay.

STARTER = CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD

🔌 CONTACTORswitches the motor
+
🛡️ OVERLOAD RELAYprotects the motor
=
⚙️ MOTOR STARTERthe assembly

Short-circuit protection (breaker or fuse) lives upstream of the starter, never inside it.

This training breaks each device down individually first, then shows how they work together. After this you'll never call a contactor a starter again — and you'll know exactly what each customer is really asking for when they say "I need a starter."

💡 POWER-UP: If you understand contactors and overloads individually, starters, soft starters, and even VFD packages all become easier.

🔌 WHAT A CONTACTOR IS

An electrically
controlled switch.

A contactor is an electrically controlled switch used to do one job, three ways:

  • ▸Turn a motor ON
  • ▸Turn a motor OFF
  • ▸Do it remotely and repeatedly

📌 The one-line description

A contactor is basically a heavy-duty relay designed for motors. Same idea as a control relay — a coil moves a set of contacts — but built to carry motor-level current thousands of times a day without burning out.

🚨 What it does NOT do

A contactor's job is switching, not protection. It will happily switch power to a motor that's seconds away from burning up. That's why another device is always required — the overload relay.

💡 POWER-UP: "The contactor handles switching the motor on and off." That single sentence makes you sound knowledgeable on every motor-control call.

🔌 INSIDE A CONTACTOR

Coil. Contacts.
That's the whole magic trick.

Every contactor — from a tiny IEC mini to a giant 500A NEMA Size 6 — has the same three internal parts.

🧲
THE COIL
CONTROL CIRCUIT INPUT · LOW POWER
An electromagnet. Energize the coil with control voltage (24V, 120V, 230V, etc.) and it pulls the main contacts closed. De-energize it and a spring pulls them open. The coil's only job is to move the contacts.
⚡
THE MAIN CONTACTS
POWER CIRCUIT · HIGH CURRENT
Big, beefy contacts that carry the motor's actual current. Sized in amps and HP. Three contacts for a three-phase motor — one for each line.
🔁
AUXILIARY CONTACTS
CONTROL CIRCUIT · SIGNALING
Small, signal-only contacts that open or close in sync with the main contacts. Used for: seal-in logic, pilot lights, "running" feedback to a PLC, and interlocking with the overload relay.

⚡ THE SEQUENCE

COIL ENERGIZEDcontrol voltage in
→
MAIN CONTACTS CLOSEpower flows
→
MOTOR RUNSuntil coil drops out
💡 POWER-UP: "Coil pulls in, contacts close, motor runs. Coil drops out, contacts open, motor stops." That's the entire mental model.

🔌 CONTACTOR RATINGS THAT MATTER

Four numbers.
Every quote.

When selecting or selling a contactor, four ratings drive the SKU. Miss any one and the part is wrong — even if everything else fits.

RatingWhat it meansQuestion to ask
Coil voltageVoltage that energizes the coil (24V, 120V, 230V, 480V — AC or DC)"What's your control circuit voltage?"
Current / HPHow much motor current the main contacts can switch — usually listed by HP at a given voltage"What's the motor HP and FLA?"
Duty ratingHow the contactor is used — see AC-1, AC-3, AC-4 below"Standard motor starting? Any plugging or jogging?"
PolesNumber of switched conductors — most three-phase motors use 3-pole"Three-phase or single-phase?"

📋 Duty rating cheat sheet

AC-1 — non-inductive / resistive loads (heaters, lighting). AC-3 — standard squirrel-cage motor starting. The default for almost every motor call. AC-4 — heavy-duty: plugging, jogging, rapid reversing. Costs more.

💡 POWER-UP: Coil voltage is the most common mis-quote. Always confirm it. A 120V coil on a 24V control circuit will sit there cold and do nothing.

🔌 WHAT A CONTACTOR DOES NOT DO

A switch is not
a guardian.

This is the single most-confused point in motor-control sales. A contactor handles switching. It does not handle protection. Anyone who tells you otherwise is wrong, and a customer who buys "just a contactor" is buying an unfinished system.

✅ WHAT IT DOES

  • ✓ Switches motor power ON and OFF
  • ✓ Switches the motor remotely (control coil)
  • ✓ Switches the motor repeatedly without wear
  • ✓ Sized to carry rated motor current

🚫 WHAT IT DOES NOT

  • ✗ Does NOT protect against overloads
  • ✗ Does NOT interrupt short circuits
  • ✗ Does NOT sense motor temperature
  • ✗ Does NOT detect phase loss

🧠 Why this matters in sales

If a customer says "I need a starter, just send me the contactor," stop and ask the follow-up. They either need the overload too (most likely), or they already have one and you should confirm the existing overload is sized correctly for the new contactor.

💡 POWER-UP: "The contactor switches. The overload protects. They're a pair." Use this line every time someone calls a contactor a starter.

🛡️ WHAT AN OVERLOAD RELAY IS

The motor's
thermal bodyguard.

An overload relay is a motor-protection device. Its job is to keep the motor from cooking itself when something downstream goes wrong — a jammed conveyor, a seized bearing, a lost phase. It watches motor current and trips when current stays too high for too long.

🛡️
OVERLOAD RELAY · WHAT IT PROTECTS AGAINST
Three kinds of trouble
  • Mechanical overloads — jam, drag, oversized load. Motor pulls too much current trying to push the work.
  • Phase loss — one of three phases drops out. Motor draws huge current on the remaining phases and overheats fast. (Electronic overloads detect this directly; thermals catch it via the current rise.)
  • Sustained overcurrent — any condition where the motor is drawing above its rated amps for an extended period.

🚨 What it does NOT do

An overload relay does NOT interrupt short circuits. That's a different problem — different physics, different device. Short-circuit protection is the breaker or fuse upstream of the starter. If you forget that, the overload contacts will weld shut during a fault and the motor will burn anyway.

💡 POWER-UP: "The overload protects against slow-cooker problems. The breaker protects against blow-up problems. Both have to be there."

🛡️ INVERSE-TIME PROTECTION

Why overloads
don't trip at startup.

Every motor draws 6–8× its full-load amps for the first few seconds of starting. If an overload tripped on that, no motor would ever run. So overloads are built to be patient — they trip on the combination of how much over-current and how long it lasts.

⏱ The inverse-time idea

Mild overload (110% of FLA) → may take minutes to trip.
Heavy overload (300% of FLA) → trips in seconds.
Short circuit (5000% of FLA) → the overload does not respond fast enough. That's why the upstream breaker has to handle it.

💡 POWER-UP: Inverse-time means "the bigger the overload, the faster the trip." It's why a motor can start without nuisance-tripping but won't run with a jammed shaft.

🛠️ OVERLOAD SIZING · CRITICAL SALES KNOWLEDGE

Size by FLA.
Never by HP alone.

This is the most-asked, most-mis-answered sizing question on the phone. Memorize the rule, memorize the steps, and your overload quotes stop coming back.

📋 The 3-step rule

1. Find the motor's Full Load Amps (FLA) on the nameplate.
2. Pick an overload whose adjustable range covers that FLA.
3. Set the dial to the FLA reading — and confirm the trip class matches the load type.

🚫 UNDERSIZED

  • ✗ Trips on every motor start ("nuisance trips")
  • ✗ Reps swap to a bigger overload to make it stop
  • ✗ Motor is now under-protected — back to square one
  • ✗ Customer thinks the part is defective

🔥 OVERSIZED

  • ✗ Motor cooks before the overload notices
  • ✗ Insurance / warranty argument afterwards
  • ✗ "I sized it to be safe" is the cause
  • ✗ The most expensive mistake in motor control

⚠️ Why "size by HP alone" fails

Two motors with the same HP can have different FLA depending on voltage, phase, efficiency, and service factor. The nameplate tells the truth. The HP rating only narrows the range.

💡 POWER-UP: "Send me the motor nameplate" beats "send me the HP" every time. Get the FLA.

🛠️ TRIP CLASSES

Class 10. Class 20.
Class 30.

Trip class tells you how long the overload will tolerate a 600% over-current before it trips. Higher class = more patience = better for loads that take a long time to come up to speed.

ClassTrip @ 600% FLA in…Use it for
Class 10≤ 10 secondsStandard motors. Pumps, fans, conveyors with normal start. The default.
Class 20≤ 20 secondsHigher inertia loads. Long-shaft fans, loaded conveyors, mixer/agitator loads.
Class 30≤ 30 secondsVery high inertia or long acceleration. Centrifuges, large fans, hammer mills.

📞 Customer signals

"It nuisance-trips every time we start it" with the load type matching a higher-inertia application → step up the trip class. Class 10 → Class 20 is the most common fix. "We have a long-shaft fan" or "big centrifuge" → start at Class 20 or 30. Don't wait for the customer to come back angry.

🚨 The trap

Class 30 looks "safer" — it never trips on startup. But it also takes three times longer to react to a real overload. Don't put a Class 30 on a standard pump just because Class 10 was nuisance-tripping. Find the real cause first (FLA mis-set, bearings, voltage drop, etc.).

💡 POWER-UP: Class 10 standard · Class 20 high inertia · Class 30 very high inertia. Three numbers, one matched answer.

🛠️ WHAT A STARTER REALLY IS

Two devices, three jobs,
one assembly.

Now that you understand each piece, the definition of a motor starter falls right out of the parts list.

⚙️ MOTOR STARTER = CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD RELAY

(with short-circuit protection always living upstream)

A "starter" may also include:

  • ▸Disconnect switch — a means to isolate the assembly for service.
  • ▸Fuses or circuit breaker — the short-circuit protection mentioned above, often packaged in the same enclosure.
  • ▸Enclosure — NEMA 1, 4, 4X, 7, 12 depending on environment.
  • ▸Control transformer — to drop service voltage down to the contactor's coil voltage (e.g. 480 → 120 VAC).
  • ▸Operator devices — Start/Stop pushbuttons, pilot lights, selector switches.

📌 At the core

Contactor = switching. Overload = motor protection. Everything else on a starter (disconnect, breaker, enclosure, transformer, operator devices) is convenience packaging around those two functions.

💡 POWER-UP: When a customer says "I need a starter," ask: "Combo unit with disconnect and breaker, or just the contactor + overload?" That one follow-up tells you whether it's a $200 quote or a $2,000 quote.

🛠️ 5 SALES MISTAKES TO AVOID

Each one costs
a callback.

Every mistake on this list has cost a rep an order, a returned part, or — worst case — a burned-up motor. Memorize and avoid.

1

Calling a contactor a starter.

A contactor is one piece of a starter. The customer might still need the overload, the disconnect, and the breaker. Ask before you ship.

2

Forgetting overload protection.

If the customer only orders a contactor and runs it as a starter, the motor is unprotected. The first jam burns it up. Always confirm the overload story.

3

Oversizing the overload "to be safe."

An oversized overload reads "safe" and acts unsafe — it won't trip until the motor is cooked. Size to FLA. Period.

4

Ignoring trip class.

Selling a Class 10 to a centrifuge customer means nuisance trips for life. Selling a Class 30 to a small pump means the motor's gone before the OL notices.

5

Mixing IEC and NEMA without checking.

The mounting and current ratings don't match 1-for-1. Swapping brands or formats without verifying the cross-reference gets the install wrong every time.

🔌 What each device does

Contactor = switches motor ON/OFF (does NOT protect). Overload relay = protects motor from overheating (does NOT interrupt short circuits). Breaker/fuse = upstream short-circuit protection.

⚙️ What a starter really is

STARTER = CONTACTOR + OVERLOAD, with short-circuit protection upstream. May also include disconnect, breaker, enclosure, control transformer, operator devices.

📞 The 5 first questions

1) Motor voltage, HP, FLA · 2) Control coil voltage · 3) Load type (normal / high inertia) · 4) Start frequency · 5) NEMA or IEC preference

🛠️ Contactor selection

✔ Coil voltage = control circuit voltage · ✔ HP & current rating ≥ motor · ✔ AC-3 duty for standard motors · ✔ 3 poles for three-phase. ❌ Never assume coil voltage.

🛡️ Overload selection

✔ Size to motor FLA (not HP) · ✔ Trip class matches the load (10 / 20 / 30) · ✔ Adjustable range covers the FLA. ❌ Never oversize "to be safe."

⏱ Trip class quick guide

Class 10 standard motors (default) · Class 20 higher inertia · Class 30 very high inertia / long start.

🌡️ Thermal vs Electronic

Thermal = simple, reliable, bimetal, no diagnostics. Electronic = precise, phase-loss detection, adjustable trip class, comms options.

🚨 5 Mistakes to Avoid

Calling a contactor a starter · Forgetting overload protection · Oversizing overloads · Ignoring trip class · Mixing IEC and NEMA without checking equivalency.

🧠 The Memory Hook

The contactor switches it. The overload protects it. Together they make the starter.
