📖 FOUNDATION · WHAT IS A REACTOR?

A speed bump
for electricity.

A reactor is simply an inductor — a coil of wire wound around an iron core. Its job: resist rapid changes in current. That one property solves two completely different problems at two different points in a VFD system.

⚡ The Physics (in plain English)

When current tries to change quickly — whether a voltage spike arriving from the utility or a reflected wave bouncing back from a motor — the inductor generates a voltage that opposes that change (Lenz's Law). The faster the current tries to change, the harder the reactor pushes back.

Voltage Spike
arrives fast
→
Reactor Coil
opposes change
→
Smoothed Waveform
reaches load
🔵
Line Reactor
Input Side · Before VFD
Impedance Options
  • 3% impedance — standard choice, lighter harmonic reduction, minimal voltage drop
  • 5% impedance — stronger protection, more harmonic suppression, small voltage drop (~5V on 480V)
🟢
Load Reactor
Output Side · Between VFD & Motor
Key Action
  • Slows the rate of voltage rise (dv/dt) hitting motor terminals
  • Blocks reflected wave energy from doubling at motor windings

📖 FOUNDATION · LINE REACTOR DEEP DIVE

The VFD's
bodyguard.

A line reactor sits between the incoming power and the VFD. If the problem starts on the power side of the drive, this is the first accessory to think about.

⚡
What a Line Reactor Does
Installed before the VFD
Smooths incoming power • reduces harmonics • reduces inrush current • reduces voltage spikes • helps protect the drive from dirty power
Line-Reactor ClueWhy It Matters
Generator, weak utility, or dirty incoming powerBuffers the drive from unstable power and helps calm surges before they hit the VFD.
Multiple VFDs on one systemHelps smooth the pulsed current draw that raises harmonics and interference.
Sensitive facilitiesHospitals, data centers, and automation-heavy plants care more about clean power and nuisance issues.
Nuisance tripping or DC bus stressOften an input-side clue that the drive needs buffering on the line side.
Large transformer very close to the VFDAvailable fault current and stress rise fast; the reactor helps buffer the drive.

📌 3% vs 5% — Which to Specify

3% → Most common. General protection for standard applications.
5% → Better harmonic reduction, more protection, and more voltage drop. Use for difficult power systems, high harmonics, generator-fed jobs, or sensitive installations.

Sales shortcut: "If the problem is on the power side of the drive, think LINE reactor."

📖 FOUNDATION · LOAD REACTOR DEEP DIVE

The motor's
shock absorber.

A load reactor sits between the VFD output and the motor. It protects motor insulation, the motor cable, and the motor itself from the fast PWM pulses coming out of the drive.

🔌 Why the Cable Length Matters

A VFD doesn't output a true sine wave. It outputs a series of fast PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) pulses. When those pulses travel down a long cable, the cable acts like a transmission line. At the motor end, there's an impedance mismatch — and the voltage pulse reflects back, arriving at the motor terminals at nearly double the original amplitude.

VFD Output
PWM pulses
→
Long Cable
transmission line
→
Motor Terminals
2× voltage!
⚠️
Without Load Reactor
  • Sharp voltage spikes hammer motor windings on long runs
  • Reflected wave stress builds heat and insulation damage
  • Older or standard motors are the first to complain
✅
With Load Reactor
  • Slows rate of voltage rise (dv/dt)
  • Reduces reflected wave stress at the motor terminals
  • Smooths the PWM waveform and helps extend motor life

📌 Fast Sales Read

"If the problem is between the drive and motor, think LOAD reactor."

📖 COMPATIBILITY · REFLECTED WAVE EXPLAINED

The voltage that
doubles back.

Reflected wave is the single most important concept in load reactor sales. Once you can explain this in 30 seconds, you'll close every accessory conversation.

1️⃣
The VFD outputs PWM pulses
A VFD doesn't produce a clean sine wave. It produces rapid on/off voltage pulses (PWM) — typically switching thousands of times per second. These pulses have very steep voltage rise times (high dv/dt).
2️⃣
The cable acts as a transmission line
When a fast pulse travels down a cable, the cable has a characteristic "surge impedance." If the cable is long enough, the pulse behaves like a wave traveling down a wire — not just a simple voltage.
3️⃣
Impedance mismatch at the motor
The motor's terminal impedance is very different from the cable's surge impedance. When the voltage wave hits this mismatch, it reflects back — and the reflected wave adds to the incoming pulse, nearly doubling the voltage at motor terminals.

💬 30-Second Customer Explanation

"Your VFD sends fast voltage pulses down that cable. On a long run, the cable behaves like a transmission line and part of the pulse reflects back at the motor like an echo. A load reactor slows that pulse down so the motor insulation takes a lot less abuse."

📖 COMPATIBILITY · MOTOR VULNERABILITY

Not all motors
are equal on a VFD.

The motor's tolerance to reflected wave depends on how it was built. Two motors can look identical on the outside — and have very different insulation systems inside.

✅ Inverter-Duty Motor

  • Built specifically for VFD use
  • Stronger insulation system for PWM output
  • Can usually handle longer runs better than a standard motor
  • Still needs protection at longer distances
  • Distance and cable conditions still matter

⚠️ Standard TEFC Motor

  • Conventional winding insulation — not designed for PWM
  • Lower tolerance to rapid voltage rise
  • Especially vulnerable: older motors and rewound motors
  • Lower threshold for recommending protection
  • Watch closely once cable length starts climbing
⚠️ The Most Common Mistake in Motor/VFD Sales

Selling a VFD with a standard or older motor on a long cable run — and no output protection. The motor fails early, the customer blames the drive, and the whole installation looks bad. Always ask: "How far is the motor from the drive?"

📖 COMPATIBILITY · HARMONICS & IMPEDANCE

The noise your VFD
puts back on the line.

Every VFD distorts the current it draws from the utility. That distortion — called harmonic distortion — can cause interference with other equipment on the same service. A line reactor is the first and easiest fix.

🎵
What Are Harmonics?
A pure 60 Hz power supply is a single, clean sine wave. VFDs draw current in choppy, non-sinusoidal pulses — which contain additional frequencies riding on top of the normal power wave. These extra frequencies are called harmonics. They cause heat in wiring, flicker in lights, errors in meters, and nuisance trips in other drives.
📉
How a Line Reactor Helps
The line reactor's inductance smooths the pulsed current draw, reducing harmonic spikes and making life easier for the rest of the power system. A 5% reactor gives stronger smoothing than a 3% reactor, but with more voltage drop.
ImpedanceHarmonic ReductionVoltage DropBest For
3%Standard reductionLower voltage dropMost common, standard protection
5%Stronger reductionMore voltage dropGenerator power, difficult systems, higher harmonics, sensitive installs

📌 When to Specify 5%

Generator-fed systems • Difficult or dirty power • Multiple drives on the same service • Sensitive facilities or electronics nearby • Customer complaints that point to stronger input-side protection

🛠️ ACCESSORIES · dv/dt FILTERS

When a reactor
isn't enough.

A load reactor slows voltage rise. A dv/dt filter slows it much more aggressively — and it's the right call when cable runs push past 250 feet.

⚡
What Is a dv/dt Filter?
Output Side · 250–500 ft Cable Runs
dv/dt (pronounced "dee-vee dee-tee") is the mathematical notation for the rate of voltage change over time. A dv/dt filter is a more sophisticated LC (inductor-capacitor) circuit that limits the steepness of voltage rise in the PWM pulses far more effectively than a simple load reactor. It's installed on the VFD output side, same location as a load reactor.
✅
When to Use dv/dt
  • Motor cable run: 250–500 feet
  • Standard TEFC motor at 100–250 ft (borderline)
  • Customer has had motor insulation failures with only a load reactor
  • High carrier frequency settings on the VFD
📊
vs Load Reactor
  • More components (L + C) → more aggressive filtering
  • Higher cost, larger physical size
  • Tuned to specific VFD carrier frequency ranges
  • Not a substitute for a sine wave filter at 500+ ft

💬 Customer Conversation

"Your cable run is 350 feet — that's past what a standard load reactor covers well. We'd recommend a dv/dt filter. It's more aggressive voltage protection and it'll keep the motor healthy at that distance."

🛠️ ACCESSORIES · SINE WAVE FILTERS

The cleanest
output possible.

A sine wave filter reconstitutes a near-true sine wave from the VFD's PWM output. It eliminates reflected wave problems entirely — and it's the only practical solution for very long cable runs.

🌊
What Is a Sine Wave Filter?
Output Side · 500+ ft Cable Runs
A sine wave filter is a more complete LC filter tuned to reconstruct a smooth, near-sinusoidal voltage from the drive's pulsed output. The motor "sees" something very close to a normal utility supply — eliminating reflected wave, reducing audible motor noise, and virtually eliminating insulation stress from dv/dt.
AccessoryCable RangeWhat It DoesCost Tier
Load Reactor100–250 ftSlows voltage rise (basic)$

Sine Wave Filter500+ ftReconstructs true sine wave$$$
✅
Pros
  • Motor sees near-sinusoidal voltage
  • Any motor (even standard) is fully protected
  • Eliminates reflected wave completely
  • Reduces motor audible noise
⚠️
Trade-offs
  • Highest cost of the three output accessories
  • Largest physical size
  • Some efficiency trade-off
  • Must be matched to VFD switching frequency

🛠️ APPLICATION · QUALIFYING QUESTIONS

Seven questions.
Every reactor call.

The source training pack keeps coming back to the same first questions. Ask them in this order and the right recommendation usually surfaces fast.

QuestionWhy It Matters
1. Is the issue before or after the drive?Before the drive points toward line-reactor logic. After the drive points toward load-reactor or output-filter logic.
2. How long is the motor cable?The most important output-side number. This decides whether you stay with none, quote a load reactor, or step up to filters.
3. Are there multiple drives on the system?That is a classic input-side harmonic clue for a line reactor.
4. Is incoming power unstable?Generator power, weak utility, and dirty power all push the conversation toward line-reactor protection.
5. Is it an older motor?Older and rewound motors are more vulnerable to PWM stress and reflected wave.
6. Is nuisance tripping happening?Unexpected trips often point to input-side stress that deserves a line reactor.
7. Is the motor inverter-duty rated?Motor type changes how much output-side protection you should recommend at a given cable length.

🛠️ REACTOR QUICK DECISION PATH

Six questions.
One reactor spec.

Run this path on every VFD + motor call. Each answer either closes the input-side story or the output-side story — or both.

1. Is the VFD fed by a generator, weak utility, or unstable incoming power?
YES → Start with line-reactor logic. 5% is the stronger call when the power side is clearly difficult.
NO → Move to Q2 and keep checking whether the input side still needs protection.
2. Are there multiple drives, sensitive electronics, nuisance trips, or a large transformer very close to the VFD?
YES → Recommend a line reactor. Those are all classic input-side clues.
NO → Input side may be simple enough to leave alone if the utility is clean.
3. How far is the motor from the VFD?
Under 50 ft → Usually fine, especially with a modern inverter-duty motor.
50–100 ft → Watch the application closely, especially with older or standard motors.
4. Is cable run 100–250 ft?
YES → Load reactor. Standard call — quote it automatically.
NO → Continue to Q5
5. Is cable run 250–500 ft?
YES → dv/dt filter. This is the medium-long lead zone where a simple reactor is often no longer enough.
NO → Continue to Q6
6. Is cable run over 500 ft?
YES → Sine wave filter. This is the strongest output-side solution for very long motor leads.
NO → Finish by checking motor type; older or non-inverter-duty motors justify protection sooner.
