⚡ VOLTAGE = PRESSURE

Voltage (V) —
the push.

How hard the electricity is being shoved through the wire. Measured in volts.

In sales terms:

  • ▸Higher voltage = more force. Industrial machines run on more force than houseplants.
  • ▸Voltage is the first thing you ask. Wrong voltage = wrong product. Always.
  • ▸Common voltages: 24 VDC, 120 VAC, 240 VAC, 480 VAC.
  • ▸Symbol: V. Sometimes E (for "electromotive force").

🎯 What customers say

"I need 240." · "It's a 480 system." · "Can you spec a 24-volt power supply?"

That number is voltage. Always confirm AC or DC.

💡 POWER-UP: "What voltage?" is question #1. Always. No exceptions.
+10 XP

▸ LEARN · 03 OF 15
⚡ CURRENT = FLOW

Current (I) —
how much is moving.

The volume of electricity flowing past a point per second. Measured in amps (A).

⚡ The number that matters

Amps tell you how much work the wire is doing. More amps = thicker wire, bigger breaker, more heat.

📏 Common ranges

Sensor: 0.1 A. PLC: 1–2 A. Pilot light: 0.05 A. Small motor: 5–20 A. Big motor: 100+ A. House service: 200 A.

🚨 Why current matters in sales

Wires, breakers, contactors, fuses, and disconnects are all sized to amps. If the customer says "10 amp circuit," they're telling you what to spec.

💡 POWER-UP: "What's the current draw?" is question #2. Right after voltage.
+10 XP

▸ LEARN · 04 OF 15
⚡ RESISTANCE = THE PIPE

Resistance (R) —
what fights the flow.

How much the pipe pushes back against the water. Measured in ohms (Ω).

Three things to know:

  • 1Conductors (copper, aluminum) → low resistance, electricity flows easily.
  • 2Insulators (plastic, rubber, air) → high resistance, electricity is blocked.
  • 3Resistors are components that intentionally restrict flow.
💡 The wire's job: be a great conductor. The insulation's job: be a great insulator.

🌡️ Heat happens here

When current flows through resistance, energy turns into heat. Too much current through a too-resistive wire = fire. That's the whole reason breakers and fuses exist.

💡 POWER-UP: Bad connections = high resistance = hot spots = burned panels. Tightness matters.

▸ LEARN · 05 OF 15
⚡ OHM'S LAW · THE MASTER EQUATION

V = I × R

Three letters. One equation. The Rosetta Stone of electricity.

V = I × R

Cover V → multiply

I = V ÷ R

Cover I → divide

R = V ÷ I

Cover R → divide

📞 Real-world example

Customer says: "I've got a 120V circuit and a 12-ohm load. What current does it draw?"

I = 120 ÷ 12 = 10 amps

Now you know to spec at least a 15A breaker (NEC says 125% margin).

💡 POWER-UP: If you know any two — voltage, current, resistance — you know the third. That's the whole superpower.
+10 XP

▸ LEARN · 06 OF 15
⚡ POWER · WHAT GETS PAID FOR

P = V × I

Power is the actual work being done. Measured in watts (W). It's what your electric bill charges for.

  • ▸Watts = volts × amps. Always.
  • ▸1,000 watts = 1 kilowatt (kW).
  • ▸Horsepower for motors: 1 HP ≈ 746 watts.
  • ▸Big motors are sized in HP. Power supplies are sized in watts. Transformers in VA / kVA.

🧮 Quick conversions reps need

1 HP ≈ 746 W
1 kW = 1.34 HP
1 kVA ≈ 1 kW (rough)
💡 POWER-UP: If a customer gives you any two of {V, A, W}, you can derive the third. Same trick as Ohm's Law.

⚡ AC vs DC · TWO FLAVORS

The wave vs.
the line.

AC — Alternating Current

What: Direction reverses 60 times per second (60 Hz in the US).

Where: Wall outlets. Buildings. Motors. Transformers. Anything coming from the utility.

Why: Easy to step up/down with transformers → easy to ship long distances.

Examples: 120 VAC, 240 VAC, 480 VAC.

DC — Direct Current

What: Flows in one direction. Steady.

Where: Batteries. PLCs. Sensors. Solar panels. Most electronics.

Why: Simpler. Electronics need it. Doesn't cross zero (matters for breakers).

Examples: 12 VDC, 24 VDC, 48 VDC.

🚨 Sales rule

A breaker rated 480 VAC may only handle 60 VDC. AC and DC are not interchangeable. Always confirm both — voltage AND type.

💡 POWER-UP: Wall = AC. Battery = DC. Electronics inside the wall device = DC (after a power supply converts it).

⚡ THE FOUR VOLTAGES YOU'LL HEAR

120 / 240 / 480 / 24DC

99% of the calls you take involve one of these four. Memorize the personality of each.

120 VAC

SINGLE PHASE

The household voltage. Outlets, lights, small tools, controls. One hot + one neutral + one ground.

▸ Pilot lights · pushbuttons · small relays · small AC fans

240 VAC

SPLIT PHASE

The big-appliance voltage. Two hots + neutral + ground. Common on residential dryers, water heaters, light commercial.

▸ Small motors · HVAC · packaged equipment

480 VAC

3 PHASE

The industrial voltage. Three hots + (sometimes) neutral + ground. Three-phase. Found in factories, plants, big buildings.

▸ Industrial motors · big drives · plant power

24 VDC

CONTROL

The control-circuit voltage. Inside the panel. Powers the brains: PLCs, sensors, instrumentation, HMIs.

▸ PLC I/O · sensors · prox switches · indicator lights

💡 POWER-UP: Hear "control circuit" → think 24 VDC. Hear "industrial motor" → think 480 VAC. Hear "outlet" → think 120 VAC.

▸ LEARN · 09 OF 15
⚡ SINGLE PHASE vs THREE PHASE

One DJ vs.
three taking turns.

Picture a party. Single-phase = one DJ; the music dips when they cough. Three-phase = three DJs taking turns; there's always someone playing.

SINGLE PHASE

One wave. Power dips to zero 120x per second.

→ Houses · small commercial · light loads

2 wires + ground

THREE PHASE

Three waves staggered. Power never dips to zero.

→ Factories · industrial motors · big buildings

3 hots + (optional) neutral + ground

🤔 Why does anyone care?

Three-phase power runs motors better. Smoother torque, less vibration, smaller wires for the same power. That's why every factory in America runs on three-phase.

💡 POWER-UP: "Single-phase" = 2 hot wires (or 1 hot + neutral). "Three-phase" = 3 hot wires. Count the hots — you've got the answer.

⚡ THE THREE WIRES

Hot. Neutral. Ground.

Three wires. Three jobs. Three colors. Reps confuse them constantly. After this page you won't.

🔥

HOT

BLACK / RED / BLUE

Carries the energy in. The "live" wire. This is what shocks you.

↩️

NEUTRAL

WHITE / GRAY

Carries the energy back. Completes the circuit. Normally near zero volts.

⏚

GROUND

GREEN / BARE

Safety wire. Carries fault current to earth so the breaker can trip — not your customer.

🧠 The mental model

Hot = the freeway in. Neutral = the freeway back. Ground = the emergency exit if something goes wrong.

💡 POWER-UP: Neutral and ground are NOT the same wire — even though they often end up bonded together at the panel. (More on that next page.)

▸ LEARN · 11 OF 15
⚡ GROUNDING vs BONDING

Two words. Two jobs.
One panel.

Customers and reps mix these up daily. Once you can explain the difference, you instantly sound like you know what you're doing.

🌍 GROUNDING

Connecting the system to the earth.

Lightning hits the building? The grounding rod sends it harmlessly into the dirt instead of through your panel.

Single point. Goes to the earth.

🔗 BONDING

Connecting metal parts together.

Every metal panel, conduit, and enclosure is wired together so they're all at the same voltage. A fault on any one of them trips the breaker.

All metal pieces. Tied together.

🧠 The mental model

Grounding goes DOWN (to earth). Bonding goes ACROSS (between metal parts).

💡 POWER-UP: Both protect people. Both involve green wires. Different jobs.

⚡ THE TWO WAYS THINGS GO WRONG

Short circuit vs.
Overload.

Both trip breakers. Both look like "the breaker tripped." They're not the same problem.

⚡ SHORT CIRCUIT

A wire-to-wire (or wire-to-ground) fault. Current shoots up to massive levels in milliseconds.

Cause: Bad insulation, pinched wire, water in the panel, dropped tool.

Trip speed: Instant.

🔥 OVERLOAD

More current than the wire was sized for, sustained over time. Wire heats up.

Cause: Too many things on one circuit. Stuck motor pulling 150% FLA. Aging device.

Trip speed: Slow — seconds to minutes.

🛡️ One device handles both

A standard thermal-magnetic breaker has two trip mechanisms inside: a magnetic coil for short circuits (instant), and a bimetal strip for overloads (delayed). One device. Two jobs.

💡 POWER-UP: Short = sudden + huge. Overload = slow + sustained. Same breaker handles both.

⚡ FAULT CURRENT & AIC

How big the bang
could be.

Fault current is how much current would flow during a worst-case short. AIC is how much your breaker can safely interrupt before exploding.

🚨 The rule

Breaker AIC must be ≥ available fault current. If the fault is bigger than the breaker's AIC, the breaker can't safely break it. The breaker becomes a hand grenade.

📞 Real-world numbers

Houses: ~10 kA. Light commercial: 14–22 kA. Industrial panels: 22–65 kA. Service entrances: can hit 100 kA+.

"kA" = kiloamps = thousands of amps.

🎯 What customers ask

"What's the AIC on this breaker?" — They're checking if it can survive a fault. Common AIC ratings: 5kA, 10kA, 14kA, 22kA, 65kA.

💡 POWER-UP: When in doubt, ask the customer for "available fault current at the install point." If they don't know, default to 10 kA AIC for industrial control panels.

▸ LEARN · 14 OF 15
⚡ VOLTAGE DROP

Long wire =
tired electricity.

The further electricity travels, the more voltage it loses to wire resistance. By the time it reaches the device, there might not be enough left.

📐 The NEC rule of thumb

Voltage drop should be ≤ 3% on a branch circuit and ≤ 5% total from service to load. Anything more = motors run hot, electronics misbehave, devices burn out early.

🔧 The fix

Bigger wire → less resistance → less drop. When a customer mentions a long run, they may need to upsize the conductor. That's a quote opportunity.

💡 POWER-UP: Hear "long run" or "the motor is way out at the back of the building" → ask about wire size. Voltage drop is your upsell opener.
+10 XP

▸ LEARN · 15 OF 15
⚡ WIRE SIZING (AWG)

Smaller number =
thicker wire.

American Wire Gauge (AWG) is backwards from how you'd guess. 14 AWG is small. 2 AWG is thick. 4/0 AWG ("four-aught") is enormous.

🧠 The rule

Small AWG number = thick wire = high amp capacity. 4/0 is "0000 AWG" — the biggest common copper.

📐 Sizing logic

Wire amp capacity ("ampacity") must equal or exceed the breaker rating. Otherwise the wire melts before the breaker trips.

🛠️ THE COMPONENT LINEUP

Know your roster.

The 8 components that show up on 90% of your quotes.

🔌 BREAKER

Resettable overcurrent protection. Trips on faults. UL 489 (branch) or UL 1077 (supplementary).

💥 FUSE

One-shot overcurrent protection. Cheaper, faster, but you replace it after every fault.

🔁 RELAY

Small switch with a coil. Low current. Switches signals or pilot lights. Ice cube relay = 8/11/14 pin.

⚙️ CONTACTOR

Big relay. Switches motor-level current. Has a coil, three power poles, and aux contacts.

📦 TRANSFORMER

Steps voltage up or down. 480→120 is the most common in panels. Sized in VA / kVA.

🔋 POWER SUPPLY

Converts AC to DC. Common: 120VAC → 24VDC. Sized in watts or amps DC.

🎚️ TERMINAL BLOCK

A junction. Connects field wires to panel wires. Stocked by the strip-foot.

🎛️ PLC

Programmable Logic Controller. The brain. Reads inputs, runs logic, writes outputs.

💡 POWER-UP: Customer mentions a component → know which family it's in → ask the right follow-ups.

🛠️ RELAY vs CONTACTOR

Same idea.
Different size.

Both are switches with coils. One handles signals, the other handles motors. Reps confuse them weekly.

🔁 RELAY

  • ▸ Small. Pin-base or DIN.
  • ▸ Switches up to ~10 A.
  • ▸ Used in control circuits.
  • ▸ Coil voltages: 24 VDC, 120 VAC, etc.
  • ▸ Switches: pilot lights, signals, small loads.

⚙️ CONTACTOR

  • ▸ Big. Bolts to back of panel.
  • ▸ Switches up to hundreds of amps.
  • ▸ Used for motors and big loads.
  • ▸ Coil voltages: 24 VDC, 120 VAC, 480 VAC.
  • ▸ Often paired with overload relay.

🧠 Mental shortcut

Relay = signals. Contactor = power. Or: relay = "tells the contactor what to do."

💡 POWER-UP: If the load is a motor → contactor. If the load is a signal or pilot light → relay.

🛠️ BRANCH vs SUPPLEMENTARY

UL 489 vs UL 1077.

The single most important breaker distinction. Mis-quote it and the panel fails UL inspection.

🛡️

UL 489 — BRANCH CIRCUIT

  • ● Stand-alone branch protection.
  • ● Can be the FIRST device after the service.
  • ● Higher AIC. Tougher testing.
  • ● Required at service entrance, NEC Article 240.
  • ● Examples: main breakers, panelboard breakers.
🧊

UL 1077 — SUPPLEMENTARY

  • ● Supplements an upstream device.
  • ● Must sit DOWNSTREAM of a UL 489 breaker.
  • ● Lower AIC, smaller form factor.
  • ● Inside control panels — protects individual devices.
  • ● Example: Ice cube breakers.

🚨 The test question

"Is this the FIRST device the wire hits after the service?" — Yes → UL 489. No → UL 1077 is fine.

💡 POWER-UP: 489 = first line. 1077 = second line. Never first line.

🛠️ MOTORS 101

3 numbers off the
motor nameplate.

Every motor sale starts with three numbers. Get them, get the right product.

▸ MOTOR NAMEPLATE

HORSEPOWER (HP)

5 HP

VOLTAGE

480V / 3φ

FLA (FULL LOAD AMPS)

7.6 A

SERVICE FACTOR

1.15

  • 1HP (Horsepower) — how much work the motor can do. Common: 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 HP.
  • 2Voltage + Phase — must match the panel feed. 480V three-phase is the industrial default.
  • 3FLA (Full Load Amps) — current the motor draws at rated load. Drives breaker, contactor, and overload sizing.

🔧 The sizing rule (NEC)

Motor branch breaker = 250% of FLA. A 7.6 FLA motor → 19 A → round up to a 20A breaker. (Yes, that's bigger than you'd expect — motors have inrush current.)

💡 POWER-UP: "What's on the nameplate?" beats every other motor question. Get those 4 numbers → quote anything.

🛠️ STARTER · SOFT START · VFD

Three ways to
start a motor.

🔌 STARTER

FULL VOLTAGE / "ACROSS-THE-LINE"

How: Full voltage hits the motor instantly.

Inrush: 6–8x FLA. Hard kick.

Cost: Cheapest.

Use when: Small motors, simple loads, no speed control needed.

🌊 SOFT STARTER

RAMP UP THE VOLTAGE

How: Ramps voltage up from 0 to full over a few seconds.

Inrush: Reduced. Smoother start.

Cost: Mid.

Use when: Reduce mechanical wear, high-inertia loads (conveyors, pumps).

🎛️ VFD

VARIABLE FREQUENCY DRIVE

How: Controls voltage AND frequency → controls speed.

Inrush: Minimal.

Cost: Highest.

Use when: Need speed control, energy savings, precise process control.

🧠 The rep's decision tree

Need speed control? → VFD. Just need a softer start? → Soft starter. Cheap, simple, on/off? → Starter.

💡 POWER-UP: "Do you need to vary the speed?" answers most of the question in one shot.

🛠️ READING A LADDER SCHEMATIC

Read it like a book.
Top to bottom.

Two vertical rails (left = power, right = neutral). Each "rung" is one circuit. Read each rung from left to right: "if these conditions are true, energize this output."

🚨 THE 5 RED FLAGS · ESCALATE, DON'T WING IT

When to stop and get
your team involved.

Some questions are above your pay grade — and that's a good thing. Knowing when to escalate is part of the job.

!

1 — Customer asks for service-entrance equipment over the phone.

Service-entrance, main switchgear, and utility-side equipment require formal sizing, code review, and stamped drawings. Escalate to AE.

!

2 — Customer doesn't know their voltage, phase, or fault current.

Don't guess. Ask them to send the panel schedule or the equipment nameplate. If they can't, involve a senior teammate or your AE.

!

3 — "Will this pass UL inspection?"

Be transparent: RSP Supply is not a UL-certified inspector. Let the customer know we offer engineering services to evaluate UL compliance — at an additional cost — and route them through your AE to scope it out.

!

4 — "Is this safe?" or "Is this code-compliant?"

Code compliance is the customer's licensed electrician's responsibility, not yours. Don't make the call.

!

5 — Anything involving arc flash hazard analysis or fault studies.

That's an engineering deliverable. Hand off, don't quote it.

💡 POWER-UP: "Let me get my engineer on this one" is a sign of competence, not weakness. Customers respect it.

🛠️ MISTAKES THAT KILL DEALS

Don't be that rep.

✕

1 — Quoting AC-rated equipment for a DC application.

A breaker rated 480 VAC may only handle 60 VDC. Always confirm AC vs. DC.

✕

2 — Sizing a motor breaker to FLA only.

Motor inrush is 6–8x FLA. Branch breaker should be ~250% of FLA. Otherwise it nuisance-trips on every start.

✕

3 — Ignoring AIC.

Spec a 6 kA breaker on a 22 kA bus and you've sold the customer a hand grenade.

✕

4 — Confusing single-phase 240V with three-phase 240V.

Two completely different beasts. Always confirm phase count.

✕

5 — Forgetting voltage drop on long runs.

500 ft of undersized wire = motor running hot. Always ask the run length.

✕

6 — Quoting UL 1077 as a branch breaker.

Will fail UL inspection. UL 1077 is supplementary only.

⚡ The water analogy

Voltage = pressure. Current = flow. Resistance = pipe width.

🧮 The two equations

V = I × R (Ohm's Law)   |   P = V × I (Power)

🔌 The 4 voltages

120 VAC outlets · 240 VAC appliances · 480 VAC industrial · 24 VDC controls

📞 The 5-question script

1) Voltage + phase?   2) Current/load?   3) Powering what?   4) Upstream protection?   5) Mount + space?

🧊 UL 489 vs UL 1077

489 = first device after service · 1077 = downstream supplementary only

⚙️ Relay vs contactor

Relay = signals · Contactor = motors

🚨 Always confirm

AC vs DC · Single-phase vs three-phase · AIC ≥ available fault current

🧠 The memory hook

Pressure pushes. Flow flows. Pipe restricts. The rest is math.
